1. Title

Two-layer wearable display garment comprising a launderable body-side inner garment and a sealed, non-launderable outer display shell, and a platform for authoring, validating, distributing and rendering user-programmable garment content.

2. Field

[0001]The invention relates to wearable electronics and apparel. More specifically, it relates to garments whose outer surface is substantially an addressable display; to the mechanical, electrical, thermal and environmental interfaces between a display layer and a body-contacting layer; and to software systems for creating, checking, selling and rendering visual content on such garments.

3. Background and prior art landscape

[0002]This section summarizes publicly known technology the inventor is aware of. It is not the result of a professional prior-art search, and one should be done before any filing.

3.1 Electroluminescent and LED apparel

[0003]Sound-reactive T-shirts with a removable electroluminescent (EL) panel and a pocket battery pack were sold widely in the mid-2000s. EL wire and EL panels are common in costumes and stage wear. Garments with LEDs sewn in or mounted behind fabric have been shown by fashion-technology studios, including dresses covered in LEDs, and by electronics companies, including light-emitting textile panels demonstrated in the mid-2000s. Light-up footwear has long been sold as a consumer product. In these products, the light source is usually fixed-function or low-resolution, the electronics sit inside a fabric garment that has to be washed, and the graphics are mostly set at the factory.

3.2 Connected and interactive textiles

[0004]Interactive garments such as touch-sensitive jacket cuffs woven with conductive yarn have reached the market. They typically use a small, removable electronics tag so the garment can be washed. Heated jackets and vests use removable battery packs. Three-in-one outerwear uses an inner liner that zips into an outer shell. These products show that removable electronics and layered garment systems are known in general.

3.3 Flexible, stretchable and fiber displays

[0005]Flexible OLED panels are in mass production for curved and foldable phones, often protected by ultra-thin glass or hard-coated polymer cover films. Display makers have shown stretchable display prototypes. Academic groups have published display textiles woven from light-emitting and conductive fibers. Flexible micro-LED and mini-LED on film, and flexible addressable LED matrices on polyimide circuits, are commercially available at low to moderate resolution.

3.4 Color-changing surfaces

[0006]Bistable electrophoretic (e-paper) films are used in readers, shelf labels and architectural panels. A color-changing e-paper car wrap has been shown at a major consumer electronics show. These surfaces draw almost no power to hold an image, but they refresh slowly and have a limited color range.

3.5 Content platforms

[0007]Watch-face stores, game cosmetic marketplaces, and print-on-demand services for mugs and apparel are all established. They let third-party creators sell designs for devices and products made by someone else.

3.6 What appears to be new here

[0008]As the inventor understands it, the combination that appears distinctive is: (a) splitting the garment into a display shell that is designed never to be laundered, and a separate body-side garment that takes on every launderable, thermal and power-storage function; (b) a single sealed dock as the only electrical path between them, together with a perimeter mechanical attachment; (c) treating the whole outer surface of the garment as a canvas mapped to the garment's pattern pieces; and (d) an open authoring system built on standard web languages and AI generation, with automatic safety, power and fit checks for each garment cut, connected to a creator marketplace with remix royalties and print-on-demand output. Whether any of these is patentable alone or in combination is a question for a professional search and an attorney.

4. Problems addressed

  1. Laundering. Washing destroys or degrades electronics, and removing them by hand before every wash is a burden that users eventually stop putting up with.
  2. Durability. Garments flex, rub, get wet and get stained. Displays need protection like a phone gets.
  3. Heat and comfort. Body heat and display heat stack up, and a sealed display surface blocks air and moisture.
  4. Locked content. Earlier products showed fixed graphics, so they got stale quickly.
  5. Appearance when off. Earlier products looked like gadgets attached to clothing.
  6. Public-display safety. A wearable screen in public raises photosensitive, driver-distraction and content concerns.

5. Summary of the invention

[0009]A wearable display system comprising an inner garment worn against the body and an outer display shell worn over it. The inner garment performs body-facing functions: moisture wicking, thermal management, collecting soil and odor, and housing a removable power and control module. It is the launderable or replaceable part. The outer shell carries flexible display elements that cover most of its visible surface, fully encapsulated under a clear protective layer, so the shell is waterproof, resists common substances and abrasion, and is maintained by wiping rather than laundering. A dock joins the two layers electrically. It is the only electrical path between them and is sealed on the shell side. A perimeter attachment joins them mechanically.

[0010]A software platform lets users author garment content (skins) in standard web languages such as HTML, CSS and SVG, and optionally shader or image-sequence formats. Content is mapped onto each garment cut's pattern pieces, run in a sandbox without network access, checked automatically for flash rate, luminance, power and fit, then compiled into a package the garment's controller can play. An AI design service generates or edits skins from natural-language requests and is metered in credits. A marketplace distributes skins under defined licenses, tracks remix lineage, pays creators, and exports designs to print-on-demand production of physical goods.

6. Figures

7. Inner garment (body-side layer)

[0011]The inner garment is a close-fitting or relaxed-fit base layer, such as a long-sleeve top, a hooded top, or a top and pant set, worn directly against the body. It is the only layer meant to touch skin, and it is designed to be machine-washed once the power and control module has been removed, or replaced cheaply.

7.1 Textile

[0012]Example materials include moisture-wicking knits (polyester, nylon or merino blends with elastane), with zones of three-dimensional spacer mesh. The spacer mesh holds the shell slightly away from the body and forms air channels. Antimicrobial or odor-control finishes may be applied. A lightweight, wipeable liner face may be placed where the inner garment meets the shell, so it doesn't shed lint onto the shell's inside surface.

7.2 Spine pack

[0013]A removable module, located between the shoulder blades or along the lower back, holds the rechargeable battery, a battery management system, the main controller, and optionally a radio, sensors and fan drivers. It sits in a padded pocket that has a thermal barrier on the body side, and it connects to the suit side of the dock through a short flexible harness that comes out with the pack before washing.

7.3 Thermal features

[0014]Channels guide warm, moist air toward exhaust areas at the underarms, side seams, back yoke and hem. In some embodiments, small blowers in the spine pack push air through the channels, or phase-change material inserts buffer heat. Section 11 covers this in detail.

7.4 Optional functions

[0015]Optional features include body-side temperature sensing, which can automatically dim the display or speed up the fans, and an emergency mode where a hard switch on the pack cuts power to the shell.

8. Outer display shell

[0016]The shell is a garment-shaped assembly with a hood or collar, body panels and sleeves. Its visible surface is covered mostly by addressable display elements laid out to follow the garment's pattern pieces. It is built as a laminate, from the outside in:

  1. Protective cover layer. A clear, hard-coated polymer film (for example polyurethane or polyimide with a hard coat) on areas that bend a lot. Ultra-thin glass is an option on flatter areas such as the back or a structured jacket front. It may carry anti-smudge (oleophobic), anti-glare and low-friction coatings, so fibers and dust don't stick.
  2. Optical layer. An optional diffuser or optical-bonding layer that softens pixel structure and joins tiles visually.
  3. Display layer. Flexible emissive panels (flexible OLED, micro-LED or mini-LED on film, or addressable LED matrices on flexible circuit board) or reflective panels (color e-paper). The display is tiled so each tile follows one pattern piece or zone, and flex zones such as elbows, shoulders and the hood hinge get narrower tiles or a lower-density display.
  4. Interconnect layer. Flexible printed circuits or stretchable conductive traces that link the tiles to the panel drivers, routed along seams and laid out in serpentine shapes where they cross flex zones.
  5. Backing and encapsulation. A sealed polymer envelope (thermoplastic polyurethane or silicone) that encloses the display and interconnects completely, bonded to a structural textile backing that gives the shell its drape and shape.

8.1 Tiled construction

[0017]In a tiled embodiment, each display tile is sealed on its own, and the tiles sit on a breathable carrier with small gaps between them. This lets the shell flex like fabric and allows air and vapor to pass through the gaps, while every electronic part stays enclosed. The rendering engine treats the gaps as dead space in the pixel map, so the image stays continuous across them.

8.2 Appearance when off

[0018]The cover layer, its color and texture, the seam trims and the cut are all designed so the shell reads as a finished garment when the display is dark. The idle look is a user setting: a dark surface, a static color held at low power on reflective displays, or a slow ambient pattern.

8.3 Care

[0019]The shell is not laundered. Care consists of wiping with a damp cloth and mild cleaner, drying, and storing it on a charging hanger. Its inside face is smooth and wipeable, because the inner garment has already captured sweat and soil.

9. Layer interface

9.1 Mechanical attachment

[0020]The shell and inner garment are joined along a perimeter at the neckline, front opening, cuffs and hem, using any of the following: a separating zipper, snaps, hook-and-loop strips, or a magnetic tape. This holds the shell in the right position over the thermal channels and keeps the dock lined up. The attachment can also be partial, leaving the shell to hang free at the hem for airflow.

9.2 The dock

[0021]Power and data pass through one dock, placed on the upper back so the pack's harness stays short. The shell side has flush contact pads, potted and sealed, with no openings, so it can be wiped and get wet. The suit side has spring-loaded pins, alignment magnets and a compressible gasket. Contacts are keyed so the dock can only connect one way, and power is only switched on once the controller confirms the connection, so the pads are never live while exposed. A breakaway design lets the dock pull apart safely under a sharp tug.

9.3 Contactless variant

[0022]In another embodiment, the dock uses inductive power transfer and a short-range, near-field data link, so neither side has any exposed metal. This costs some efficiency and adds some heat in exchange for an even better seal.

9.4 Identification

[0023]The shell carries an identity chip readable through the dock that reports its cut, size, tile layout, display type and calibration. This lets one inner garment and spine pack drive different shells, and lets the platform deliver the right version of each skin.

10. Power

[0024]A rechargeable lithium-ion pack sits in the spine pack, with a battery management system that handles cell balancing, overcurrent and over-temperature protection. Keeping the pack at or below 100 watt-hours keeps it within the size most airlines allow in carry-on bags without special approval. Charging happens through a standard USB-C connector on the pack, or with the garment on a charging hanger that connects to the shell side of the dock.

10.1 Power budget model

[0025]Runtime depends on display technology, pixel count, brightness and how bright the content is on average. The platform estimates it for each skin using a model of this form:

P_display ≈ Σ_zones ( N_pixels × P_pixel,max × D_avg ) P_total = P_display + P_controller + P_radio + P_fans Runtime ≈ ( E_battery × η_conversion ) / P_total

[0026]Illustrative calculation, not a measurement: a bench-rig shell built from about 1,000 common addressable RGB LEDs has a datasheet maximum on the order of 0.3 W per LED at full white. At an average drive level of 5% for a dark, moving design, the display alone would draw around 15 W, and a 74 Wh pack would last a few hours. The same calculation explains several design choices: dark-background skins, brightness caps, power ratings in the marketplace, emissive panels with better efficiency, and e-paper for colorways that rarely change.

11. Heat and moisture management

[0027]Heat comes from two places: the body, at all times, and the display and electronics, depending on the content. The design keeps these two heat sources apart and moves moisture out of the garment.

  • Separation. Spacer mesh in the inner garment keeps an air gap between skin and shell, so display heat isn't conducted directly into the body and body heat isn't trapped against the panels.
  • Exhaust. Channels carry moist air to vents at the underarms, side seams, back yoke and hem. In the tiled shell, air also passes through the gaps between tiles.
  • Active assist. Optional low-noise blowers in the spine pack, controlled by temperature and humidity sensors.
  • Content-aware limits. The controller lowers brightness or frame rate when the shell gets too warm, and the platform shows each skin's thermal class.
  • Buffering. Optional phase-change inserts absorb short heat peaks.

[0028]Heat and breathability are listed as the top open engineering problem in section 18. They are the reason the first prototypes are a hoodie and a jacket, which already tolerate being thicker and vented.

12. Sealing, durability and test targets

13. Data and control

[0030]The controller stores installed skin packages and plays them back, sending pixel data to the panel drivers in the shell through the dock. Bulk transfer from the phone can use Bluetooth Low Energy, or Wi-Fi when available. The controller reads the shell's identity chip, applies per-tile calibration for color and brightness uniformity, enforces brightness and thermal limits, and responds to optional inputs: an ambient light sensor for auto-brightness, a motion sensor for motion-reactive skins, and, only with explicit user permission, a microphone level reading for music-reactive skins. Raw audio is never recorded or sent anywhere. The garment has no camera.

14. Software platform

14.1 Skin package

[0031]A skin is a package with a manifest (name, creator, license, supported cuts, power and thermal class, inputs used, and remix lineage) and one or more sources: HTML, CSS, SVG, a restricted shader dialect, an image sequence or video, or vector animation. It can include different versions for different cuts.

14.2 Canvas mapping

[0032]Each cut and size has a garment canvas, a 2D layout of the pattern pieces with tile positions and dead zones, similar to a UV map in 3D graphics. Authors design against that canvas or against simple "front, back, sleeves" regions. The renderer turns the design into per-tile pixel buffers and handles seams, so a pattern can flow across the body.

14.3 Sandbox

[0033]Web-language skins are rendered in an isolated environment with no network access, no external resources and, by default, no scripting. An optional, deterministic animation API exposes only time and the declared sensor inputs. This means a skin can't track the wearer, load remote content after review, or behave differently from what was checked. The studio demo on this site works the same way, rendering each skin in a sandboxed frame with a content security policy that blocks scripts and network requests.

14.4 Validator

[0034]Before a skin can be installed or listed, a validator renders it on each target canvas and checks: flash rate against the widely used photosensitive guideline of no more than three general flashes per second, with red-flash checks; luminance against per-context ceilings, such as night and driving modes; forbidden patterns, such as imitations of emergency-vehicle lighting; power and thermal class using the model in section 10; and fit, confirming that important elements don't fall on dead zones or seams.

14.5 Compilation and playback

[0035]Validated skins are compiled into a compact form the controller can play, either pre-rendered frame loops or a vector or shader program for the on-device renderer, depending on what the controller can handle. Playback runs entirely offline.

14.6 AI design pipeline

[0036]A user describes a design in words, or with a reference image. A generation service produces a skin in the platform's languages, aimed at the user's cut, and returns it with a preview and its source. Follow-up requests edit it. Each generation or edit uses credits from the user's balance. Every result goes through the same validator as hand-written skins. Generated skins come with the source code so users can learn from them and edit them by hand.

14.7 Credits

[0037]Credits are a platform balance used for AI generation. They come with a garment, can be purchased, or are earned from marketplace sales. They are metered per request according to its complexity.

14.8 Marketplace, licensing and payouts

[0038]Creators list skins under a license (personal, remix, open, or numbered edition). The platform records a remix graph that links each derived skin to its sources, and when a derived skin sells, royalties are split along that graph according to the license terms. Payouts, identity checks and tax reporting run through an established payments processor. Edition ownership is recorded by the platform and can be transferred.

14.9 Print on demand

[0039]An export step renders a still "poster frame" from any skin whose license allows it, converts it to print color spaces, and sends it to print-on-demand partners for physical goods. A second production path makes shell variants to order: base colors, textures, printed trims and non-display panels.

15. Embodiments

  1. A. Zoned hoodie (first prototype). A hooded shell with display zones on the chest, back and upper sleeves, over a hooded inner garment with a spine pack. Thickness is acceptable.
  2. B. Structured jacket. A stiffer shell with larger, flatter panels, optionally using ultra-thin glass covers on the back and front panels, plus a collar and a front zip that runs through a display break.
  3. C. Continuous tiled shell. Small sealed tiles covering nearly the whole shell on a breathable carrier, with rendering across the gaps.
  4. D. Reflective (e-paper) shell. Color e-paper tiles for skins that are mostly still or change slowly, with very long runtime and no emitted light.
  5. E. Color-only pants. Pants in a fixed cut (stiff trousers or joggers) whose shell uses a small number of large color zones, emissive or reflective, so the user colors the pants rather than displaying detailed images.
  6. F. Contactless dock. Inductive power and near-field data with no exposed contacts on either layer.
  7. G. Suit jacket. A tailored shell where the display covers selected panels such as the lapels, back and sleeves, and the rest is traditional cloth.
  8. H. Accessory modules. Hats, hoods, bag panels or cuffs using the same dock and skin format, sharing the user's library.
  9. I. Shared inner suit. One inner garment and spine pack that drives several shells, identified through the dock.

18. Risks and open engineering problems

  1. Breathability and heat. A sealed display surface blocks vapor. The tile gaps, vents and fans in sections 8 and 11 need to be proven on a body, in warm weather.
  2. Weight and stiffness. Display laminates and batteries add mass and stiffness. Drape and comfort have to be judged on real people, not by spec.
  3. Runtime. Bright emissive displays draw a lot of power (section 10). Content rules, brightness limits and display choice all trade against each other.
  4. Flex fatigue. Interconnects at the elbows, shoulders and hood hinge are the most likely points of failure.
  5. Scratch and impact. Flexible covers scratch more easily than glass, and glass doesn't bend well. The coat has to be chosen zone by zone.
  6. Safety. This covers lithium battery safety, surface temperature limits against skin, and the photosensitive and driver-distraction risks of a bright moving display in public.
  7. Regulation. Radio certification (such as FCC rules for intentional radiators in the US), battery transport testing (UN 38.3), product safety and labeling rules, and extra rules for any product marketed to children.
  8. Repair. One dead tile shouldn't mean replacing a whole shell. Tiles need to be replaceable by a service center.
  9. Sizing. Every size of every cut needs its own tile layout and canvas.
  10. Moderation at scale. A public screen invites abuse, so automatic checks need human review and reporting behind them.
  11. Intellectual property. The prior art in section 3 may anticipate parts of this. A professional search may narrow what can be claimed.
